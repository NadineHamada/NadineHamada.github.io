What kind of information is being sent?
Answer: Classical information (a message drawn from some alphabet) vs. quantum information (an actual quantum state you're trying to preserve/transmit).


"quantum information" is a proxy word for the physical resource: "Coherence".
Quantum information basically means coherence. 
A quantum state ∣ψ⟩=α∣0⟩+β∣1⟩ isn't just "some probability of being 0, some probability of being 1" — it has a definite relative phase between 
α and β. That relative phase is coherence.
When people say "preserve the quantum state",what they're really asking for is: keep the phase relationships between the amplitudes intact.
Lose the phase, and what's left is just a classical probability distribution wearing quantum notation — the "quantumness" is gone.

"quantum information" means "an actual quantum state you're trying to preserve/transmit.
If the phase decoheres in transit, you haven't transmitted the quantum state — you've transmitted a classical mixture that merely resembles it in the diagonal (populations), which is a strictly weaker, classical thing.


Explanation:
a qubit(with Coherence)(quantum physical substrate) can carry either classical messages (Holevo's setting) 
                                                                or   quantum states (needs the qubit's **coherence** to actually be preserved end-to-end).
classical bit(no coherence) (classical physical substrate) can only ever carry classical information — there's no coherence to lose.

# we need  to identify which quantum state was sent ? or we need the state to survive (reconstruct)? 
Any time the end goal is a classical answer, even though quantum carriers were involved 
we need to know/define: which state was sent? (it s (Holevo territory))
example: 
**Quantum key distribution (QKD)** — Alice sends quantum states, Bob's job is to correctly identify which bit/basis was sent so they can extract a shared classical key. What Eve (the eavesdropper) can possibly learn is also bounded by Holevo — this is literally how QKD security proofs work.

**Reading out a quantum computation** — you ran a quantum algorithm, now you need one classical bitstring answer out. You don't need the superposition to survive after readout, you need the right label.

**Quantum-enhanced classical communication / dense coding** — using quantum carriers to push more classical bits through a channel than a classical carrier could.

**Quantum state discrimination / sensing** — is this signal state A or state B? (radar, quantum illumination, biomedical quantum sensors).

Any time the whole point is that an unknown or superposed state has to survive, not get identified.
When you need "reconstruct the actual state" (coherent information territory):

Quantum teleportation — you're moving an arbitrary, possibly unknown qubit state from A to B. If you could just "identify and re-describe" it classically you wouldn't need teleportation at all — the entire premise is that it's unknown and its coherence must be preserved.
Quantum repeaters / quantum networks — extending entanglement across distance requires the entangled state itself to stay intact hop to hop.
Quantum memory — storing a qubit to hand back later for further coherent processing (not to look at it now).
Fault-tolerant quantum computing / quantum error correction — protecting a logical qubit mid-computation, where any measurement-like collapse would destroy the computation.




Why you need to know the ceiling at all, either way — three concrete uses:

Feasibility, before you build anything. If someone proposes a protocol claiming a rate above the relevant ceiling, it's not "hard to engineer," it's physically impossible — full stop, no amount of better hardware fixes it. This is often the entire point of computing the bound: killing a bad idea cheaply, on paper, before a lab tries it.
Benchmarking real hardware against reality's actual limit, not against zero. If your real system gets 60% of the Holevo bound, that's a statement about engineering headroom — you know exactly how much is left on the table and can chase it. Without the ceiling you'd have no idea if 60% is "almost optimal" or "leaving 10x on the table."
Security guarantees that don't rely on trusting the adversary's hardware. In QKD specifically, the security proof doesn't say "we assume Eve isn't clever enough" — it says "Holevo's theorem guarantees Eve cannot extract more than X bits of information regardless of what technology she has, even ones that don't exist yet." That's a much stronger claim than ordinary engineering security, and it only works because the bound is a law of physics, not an assumption about attacker capability.
